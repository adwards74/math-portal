# 1.1b: MP3 Justification Sentence Frames

            <div class="intuition-box glass">
                <h3><i class="fas fa-quote-left"></i> Math Is an Argument</h3>
                <p>In AP Precalculus, a correct number with no justification is a <strong>lost point</strong>. Mathematical Practice 3 (MP3) demands: <em>state a claim, cite the evidence, in precise language.</em> The good news: the language is <strong>formulaic</strong>. Learn the sentence frames below and you can justify concavity, symmetry, and model choices on autopilot — exactly the way AP graders expect to read them.</p>
            </div>

            <h3>1. The Frame Library</h3>

            <div class="example-card glass" style="margin-top:15px;">
                <h4><i class="fas fa-paragraph"></i> Frame A — Concavity from Rates of Change</h4>
                <p><em>"Since the average rate of change on $[a, b]$ is <strong>[increasing/decreasing]</strong> while the function values are <strong>[increasing/decreasing]</strong>, the function is concave <strong>[up/down]</strong> on the interval."</em></p>
                <p><strong>Filled example:</strong> <em>"Since the average rate of change on $[0, 4]$ is <strong>decreasing</strong> (from 4 to 3) while the function values are <strong>increasing</strong>, the function is concave <strong>down</strong> on the interval."</em></p>
                <p>Why it works: concavity is <em>defined</em> by how the rate of change behaves. Name the rates, name the trend, conclude.</p>
            </div>

            <div class="example-card glass" style="margin-top:15px;">
                <h4><i class="fas fa-paragraph"></i> Frame B — Even-Function Symmetry</h4>
                <p><em>"Since $p$ is <strong>even</strong>, $p(-a) = p(a)$. Therefore [property at $x = a$] reflects to [mirrored property at $x = -a$]."</em></p>
                <p><strong>Filled example:</strong> <em>"Since $p$ is even, $p(-2) = p(2) = 5$, and a relative maximum at $x = 2$ reflects to a relative maximum at $x = -2$."</em></p>
                <p>Why it works: evenness is a <em>global</em> property — invoke it once, then mirror everything.</p>
            </div>

            <div class="example-card glass" style="margin-top:15px;">
                <h4><i class="fas fa-paragraph"></i> Frame C — Model Choice</h4>
                <p><em>"[Function $k$] is best modeled by a <strong>[linear/quadratic/...]</strong> function <strong>because</strong> [the diagnostic evidence]."</em></p>
                <p><strong>Filled examples:</strong></p>
                <p><em>"$k$ is best modeled by a <strong>linear</strong> function because the rate of change over consecutive equal-length input intervals is <strong>constant</strong>."</em></p>
                <p><em>"$h$ is best modeled by a <strong>quadratic</strong> function because the <strong>second differences</strong> of the outputs are approximately constant."</em></p>
                <p>The <strong>because</strong> is not optional — it's the entire point of the frame.</p>
            </div>

            <div class="mastery-box glass" style="margin:20px 0; border:1px solid var(--accent-cyan); padding:15px;">
                <h4 style="color:var(--accent-cyan);"><i class="fas fa-check-double"></i> The "Because … Therefore" Skeleton</h4>
                <p>Every MP3 justification follows one skeleton:</p>
                <p style="text-align:center; font-size:1.1em;"><strong>Because</strong> [evidence from the data/graph/table], <strong>therefore</strong> [mathematical conclusion].</p>
                <p>Before you write the final sentence, check: (1) Did I cite <em>evidence</em>, not just repeat the claim? (2) Did I use the unit's <em>vocabulary</em> (rate of change, concavity, even, second differences)? (3) Is the logic arrow pointing the right way — evidence → conclusion, never conclusion → evidence?</p>
            </div>

            <div class="tj-edge glass" style="margin-top:20px;">
                <h4><i class="fas fa-skull-crossbones"></i> AP Trap: The Right Math in the Wrong Words</h4>
                <p>Graders have seen it a hundred times: the student <em>computed</em> everything correctly, then wrote "because the graph goes up then flattens." That's <strong>colloquial</strong>, not mathematical — and it loses the justification point. The frames above exist so you never have to improvise the wording under time pressure. Memorize the frames; fill in the numbers.</p>
            </div>
